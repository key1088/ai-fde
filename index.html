4. Levels & Thresholds The most common self-assessment mistake is treating "I have used it" as "I can do it." So every level has a testable standard.

LevelNameStandardTypical evidence
L0No ExposureHaven't heard of it, or only seen the term—
L1Aware of the ConceptCan say what it is and what problem it solves, but has not done itCan explain the concept to someone else
L2Can Do with GuidanceCan follow docs or an example to get it running; needs help when something breaksHas a working demo
L3Can Own It and Explain ItCan do it from scratch unaided, handle common failures, and explain it to a client in two minutesReal project output with quantified results
L4Can Set the Standard / Teach ItCan define team conventions and selection criteria, teach others, and improve other people’s designsHas a reused standard document, or has grown people
How the bar is set: skills marked L3 are core (without them you are not an AI-FDE); L2 skills are supporting (you can be shallow, but not blank). There are no L1 items — anything worth only "being aware of" did not make the list.
A healthy distribution: 60% or more at L3 and above, and no domain below 50%. If one domain is clearly low, that is usually your bottleneck.

5. Gaps by Background AI-FDE is a hybrid role — nobody arrives with all of it. Four common backgrounds, four different gaps.

Implementation / Delivery
Starting point:Strong on business context and client communication — communication skills usually clear the bar immediately.

Most often missing: AI Engineering (Evaluation, Context Engineering, on-premise inference) and Operations Engineering (observability, cost). Used to delivering against a given design, so you hesitate when the technical approach is yours to define.
Fix: start with Evaluation — it is the only way to turn "feels good" into "the data says so", and it is the strongest evidence you can show a client.
Pre-Sales / Solution
Starting point:Storytelling and business cases come naturally; discovery and demos are the fastest to pick up.

Most often missing: hands-on depth. You can present a design but cannot build it — which leaves you exposed when engineers push back or clients drill into details.
Fix: get Python, AI engineering in Python, and a low-code platform to at least L2. You do not need to write beautiful code, but you must be able to build it and make it run.
Operations / SA
Starting point:Operations Engineering and Enterprise Integration are strengths — Linux, Docker, permissions and auditing usually start high.

Most often missing: business discovery and storytelling. You can build it but cannot sell it, and the client never sees the value — the biggest hurdle for an ops background.
Fix: practise discovery and demos deliberately, as if they were technical tasks. Record yourself running a demo and watch it back; once is usually enough to see the problem.
Engineering
Starting point:Software and AI Engineering come fastest; code-heavy skills are rarely a barrier.

The trap you fall into: stack sprawl. You enjoy building your own wheels and chasing new frameworks, and lose sight of business value and delivery cadence.
Fix: force yourself to write all nine artifacts for every project — especially the business-value one. If you cannot write it, the technology has no business value.
The most overlooked item: "Failure Boundaries & Fallbacks" under Delivery & Communication. Most people spend their effort on making the AI get it right, but what the client actually cares about is what happens when it gets it wrong. Whether your proposal spells out the fallback and the human handoff is what separates a demo artist from someone who can deliver.
